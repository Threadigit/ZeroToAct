Earnings Were Never the Question

On Tuesday Nvidia reported revenue of $115.8 billion, more than double the same quarter last year, and guided to $130 billion for the next one. By Friday its shares had fallen 23 percent on the week, the worst five days since October 2008. Anthropic raised a record sum in the largest listing ever held, opened 34 percent above its price, and gave most of it back inside two sessions. Last week the Federal Reserve said the weak joint in the AI build-out was its financing rather than its earnings.

By Tolu Adetuyi

Last week we wrote that the Fed had put the risk in the financing rather than the earnings, and that the market would mark the price of AI risk twice in public within forty-eight hours. Both events ran. Neither resolved the way our falsification note anticipated, and the outcome is more informative than either branch we wrote.

The earnings arrived, and they were extraordinary

Nvidia reported third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on 17 November, for the quarter ended 25 October. Revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103 percent on the year and 20 percent on the quarter. Data centre revenue of $107.6 billion, up 110 percent. Gross margin of 74.8 percent. Diluted earnings per share of $2.70 against a consensus near $2.47, on revenue that beat a $109.3 billion estimate. Colette Kress, interim chief executive while remaining chief financial officer, said the Vera Rubin platform was off to an explosive start.

Two details in the guidance deserve more attention than they got. The $130 billion fourth-quarter outlook assumes no data centre compute revenue from China at all, which is the bullish reading. It also contains an extra week, which flatters the sequential comparison. And gross margin is guided down to 71 to 72 percent from this quarter's 74.8, as the Vera Rubin ramp adds cost. The top line is accelerating; the margin on it is not.

The stock fell 14 percent on Friday alone to close at $125.53, down about 23 percent on the week. That is its worst week since October 2008 and its seventh consecutive weekly decline, and it took roughly $900 billion of market value with it, reported as the largest one-week loss any company has recorded.

One thing complicates reading that fall as a verdict on AI financing, and it belongs here rather than in a footnote. Reuters reported mid-week that the SEC is examining Nvidia's accounting practices. The inquiry has not been confirmed by the company or the regulator and nothing about it is established, but a reported accounting inquiry is company-specific news, and some unknown share of a 23 percent week belongs to it rather than to the sector's cost of capital.

The record raise could not hold its first trade

Anthropic priced on Wednesday at $83, above its $70 to $78 range, and upsized the deal from 825 million shares to 980 million. That raised $81.3 billion at pricing, or $93.5 billion once underwriters exercised their over-allotment, at a valuation near $1.5 trillion. On either measure it is the largest initial public offering ever completed, ahead of SpaceX's $75 billion at pricing and $85.7 billion final in June.

Last week this publication wrote that it would be the second largest. The deal was upsized and priced above its range, and it is the first. That correction belongs in the body, not a footnote.

It opened on Thursday at $111.50, 34 percent above the issue price, touched $114.99 and briefly carried a valuation above $2 trillion, then closed the session at $97.17 and finished Friday at $88.91. Above the issue price. A long way below where it started trading.

So the offering did not break issue and the earnings did not disappoint. The selling happened anyway.

What the week actually priced

Set the two side by side and the conclusion is hard to avoid. A company at the centre of the AI supply chain produced one of the strongest quarters any business has reported, and lost roughly a quarter of its market value in the same five days. A company at the centre of AI itself raised more money than any company in history, and could not hold its first trade.

Nothing in either outcome is about demand for the product. Nvidia's order book is the opposite of a problem. What changed is the market's willingness to keep funding the thing at the price being asked.

The wider tape says the same. The S&P 500 fell 8.6 percent on the week and closed Friday at 6,910.39, about 10.7 percent below its 6 October record, which puts it in correction. The Dow fell 8 percent, its worst week since March 2020, and is also in correction. The Nasdaq Composite fell 10 percent, erasing its gains for 2026. The VIX closed near 35.9 after touching 40.

Two details are worth more than the index levels. Gold fell nearly 4 percent and bitcoin dropped below $59,000, the lowest since July. In an ordinary risk-off week both of those rise. And Treasury yields fell hard: the 10-year dropped about 13 basis points on Friday to 4.92 percent, the 5-year 16 basis points to 4.70. Money left equity risk and went into government duration in the same session.

Fed governor Lisa Cook, speaking on Friday, argued that waiting to raise rates risks the committee falling behind on inflation. Markets still largely expect a quarter point in December, with the meeting on the 8th and 9th and the next inflation print not until the 10th.

What a US reader should take from this. If you hold concentrated technology exposure, this week removed the most comfortable defence of it. The argument was always that the earnings would justify the prices. The earnings arrived, they were extraordinary, and the prices fell anyway. That does not mean the build-out is finished. It means the thing setting the price is the cost and availability of capital, which is a different variable to track than quarterly results.

London got the same bill in a different envelope

The ONS published October inflation on Wednesday. CPI rose to 3.6 percent from 3.5, a twelve-month high. CPIH reached 3.7 percent and RPI 4.2. Core held at 2.8 percent and services at 3.7.

Transport made the largest upward contribution, motor fuels in particular, with transport prices up 7.5 percent. Alcohol and tobacco rose to 4.5 percent from 2.5, which the ONS links partly to the duty increase on 1 October. Food inflation eased to 0.9 percent from 1.3, and restaurants and hotels pulled the rate down. That is the third consecutive month in which fuel has done the damage to a British inflation print.

The fiscal numbers published on Friday are the part most coverage skipped. October public sector borrowing came in at £17.0 billion, below the £18.0 billion expected but 5.2 percent above October last year and the third highest October since records began in 1993. Debt interest alone was £11.2 billion, up 33.4 percent on the year and the highest for any October since monthly records started in 1997, with about £4.2 billion of it driven by inflation uprating on index-linked gilts. Public sector net debt stands at £3.1 trillion, or 95.8 percent of GDP. Retail sales volumes rose 0.7 percent on the month against expectations of no change.

Note what the index-linked figure means. Britain is paying for its own inflation twice: once at the pump, and again on the part of its debt stock that reprices with the retail price index. RPI printed 4.2 percent this week.

What a UK reader should take from this. Much of this inflation is fuel and duty rather than demand, and a rate rise cannot do anything about either, but the Bank has to answer the print in front of it and this one went up. If you have a mortgage decision in the next six weeks, assume the 17th goes against you and work backwards. The index-linked debt interest bill is the slower problem, and it gets worse every month inflation stays where it is.

Lagos did the opposite trade

While New York sold duration risk, Nigerian investors bought it.

The NBS published October inflation on 16 November. Headline fell to 14.06 percent from 14.63, food eased to 19.13 from 19.59, and core dropped to 10.78 percent from 12.95, a fall of more than two percentage points in a single month.

Two caveats travel with that. The month-on-month rate went the other way, rising to 0.43 percent from 0.06 in September, a sevenfold acceleration in the pace at which prices are actually changing. And the series was rebased to a 2024 base year, so comparisons with older readings are comparisons across two different series and should be made carefully. The annual rate is falling. Prices are not.

The bond auction on 16 November shows what investors did with the headline. The DMO offered ₦2 trillion across four reopened bonds and received ₦1.81 trillion in bids, 90.51 percent of the offer. An undersubscribed auction. It allotted ₦1.42 trillion, against ₦1.21 trillion in October, and left roughly ₦390 billion on the table.

The composition is the story. The May 2033 bond drew ₦766.40 billion against a ₦300 billion offer, a subscription of 255.47 percent, and took about 48 percent of the total allotment. The March 2028 bond drew ₦276.98 billion against a ₦700 billion offer, a subscription of 39.57 percent. Stop rates fell to 16.89 to 16.99 percent, from 16.99 to 17.29 in October.

Investors would not fund the short end and fought over the long one. That is a market positioning for rate cuts. The MPC meets on 23 and 24 November with the policy rate at 23 percent.

What a Nigerian reader should take from this. The bond market has already decided rates are coming down and has priced some of it, so the easy part of that trade has gone and the long end is where everyone already is. If you are borrowing, the direction is finally in your favour, but the MPC has to act first and the bond market has been early before. And do not read the headline as relief at the till: month-on-month prices rose seven times faster in October than in September.

Nairobi found the bid it could not find a week earlier

Kenya reopened a fifteen-year and a thirty-year bond against a KSh50 billion target, with bidding closing on 18 November. Bids came to about KSh85.55 billion, roughly 171 percent of the amount sought, and the CBK accepted about KSh52.19 billion. The fifteen-year drew KSh62.43 billion and cleared at a weighted average of 12.62 percent. The thirty-year drew KSh23.12 billion and cleared at 13.59.

That answers the question we left open last week. The switch auction on 9 November drew only 37.9 percent of its target, and we asked whether that reflected reluctance to extend or the mechanics of that particular instrument. A conventional long-dated auction covered at 171 percent says it was the mechanics. Kenyan investors will lend long when the instrument is straightforward and the price is right.

What a Kenyan reader should take from this. The government can fund itself at the long end, which reduces near-term pressure on short rates and on the shilling. The thirty-year at 13.59 percent is also a visible benchmark for what long money costs in Kenya. If you are offered a long-dated private placement or a corporate note, that is the number it has to beat before the extra risk is worth anything.

What actually happened this week

The AI build-out completed its move from a technology story to a credit story, and it did so in the cleanest way the market had available.

Last week the Fed set out the arithmetic: $680 billion of advanced computing debt issued this year, five times the 2023 figure, about 31 percent of all nonfinancial investment-grade issuance, over $1 trillion of leases not yet commenced and over $2 trillion of take-or-pay contracts and residual value guarantees. It said the risk sat in that financing.

Anyone who wanted to argue had one reply available: that the revenue would arrive and cover it. On Tuesday the revenue arrived, in the largest quantity any company has reported from this trade. The answer came back within three sessions and it was not reassuring.

Be careful about how much weight one company bears here. A reported SEC inquiry into Nvidia's accounting is company-specific and landed in the same week, and the honest position is that nobody can yet separate how much of its 23 percent belongs to that and how much to the sector's cost of capital. What is not company-specific is the rest of the tape: the S&P down 8.6 percent, the Dow down 8, the Nasdaq down 10 and gold down with them. An accounting inquiry at one chipmaker does not put two indices into correction.

What the week does not tell you is that the build-out is over. Nvidia's guidance is $130 billion for a single quarter. Anthropic raised more money than any company in history and closed the week above its issue price. Demand for the product is not in question.

What it tells you is that the market has started charging a different price for funding it, and that quarterly results are no longer the variable that moves the answer. Everything still looking for money is quoted off a higher number than it was seven days ago, whether it sits in Santa Clara, Slough or Ikeja.

The rotation is visible in the other direction too. Capital that left AI equity risk this week went into government duration, in the United States, in Nigeria and in Kenya, in the same five days.

What would prove this wrong

If Nvidia recovers most of this week's decline within a month on no new information, the selling was a positioning unwind around the Anthropic listing rather than a repricing of the sector, and the read here is too strong. If the SEC inquiry is confirmed and widens, a larger share of that 23 percent was company-specific than this edition allows, and the sector conclusion rests on the index moves alone. If Anthropic trades back above $111.50 and holds there into December, the first-day fade was an allocation artefact rather than a verdict. If large AI data centre financings close on normal terms through December, the credit channel is healthier than this week's equity market implies. If Nigeria's MPC holds on 24 November and the long bond gives back its gains, the bond market was early rather than right. And if UK inflation falls back toward 3.0 percent in the 16 December release, the fuel-driven overshoot is passing and the December rate decision will look like an overreaction.

Next move

  • CareerThe useful distinction this week is between the business and the financing of the business. Nvidia's operations are performing, which means the engineering, manufacturing and data centre jobs attached to real orders are not the ones at risk. What reprices first in a credit squeeze is anything funded by a promise rather than a contract: unannounced projects, speculative capacity, teams hired against a raise that has not closed. If you work anywhere near the AI build-out in any of these markets, the question to ask about your employer or your next one is not whether the sector is growing, because it plainly is. It is whether the specific thing you work on is paid for. In Britain, retail sales rose and borrowing came in under forecast, which is better than the mood music, though some reporting attributes the sales rise to households stocking up rather than to confidence.
  • Business owners and operatorsThe cost of capital went up this week for anything that looks like a technology bet, and it went up fastest for things not yet generating cash. If you are raising, expect sharper questions about unit economics than you got in October, and expect the round to take longer. If you supply into the data centre build, nothing about demand changed but your customers' financing conditions did, so check the funding stage of any project before you commit capacity. In Nigeria the picture is better than it has been, with the annual rate falling and the bond market pricing cuts, but month-on-month prices rose seven times faster in October than in September, so price your own inputs off the monthly series rather than the headline. In Kenya, the government just demonstrated it can fund itself at thirty years, which takes some pressure off short rates.
  • InvestorsThis week is the clearest available lesson on the difference between a good company and a good price. Nvidia reported the best quarter in its history and anyone who bought it on Monday is down roughly a quarter. If you hold AI exposure through a global broker or a dollar app, which many readers in Lagos and Nairobi now do, understand that you are holding a financing bet rather than an earnings bet. Hold two things at once on Nvidia: the quarter was extraordinary, and a reported accounting inquiry is an unquantified risk sitting on top of the sector move. Resist reading Anthropic's listing as a verdict either way: it raised a record sum and faded hard, and both are true. Closer to home, Nigeria's long bond is now crowded, with the May 2033 more than two and a half times subscribed, so the easy duration gains are behind you. Kenya's thirty-year at 13.59 percent is the cleanest benchmark on that curve this quarter.
