A Cheaper Dollar Is Not Relief

The dollar fell to a five-week low after American voters handed Congress to the Democrats. Britain turned that into the strongest sterling in a decade and raised rates anyway. Kenya used it to finally sell thirty-year paper. Nigeria, which had the most to gain, ended the week weaker.

By Tolu Adetuyi

A weaker dollar is the closest thing the world economy has to a shared tailwind. It makes imports cheaper for everyone who buys in dollars, lightens every dollar debt, and gives every other central bank room it did not have the week before. This week that tailwind arrived. Four economies did four different things with it, and one of them got nothing at all.

Washington produced gridlock, and the dollar fell

Democrats took both chambers on Tuesday. Associated Press and NBC tallies on the morning of 4 November put the Senate at 54 seats to 44, a net gain of seven including Texas, Kansas and South Carolina, with Alaska and Mississippi too close to call. The House stood at 236 to 146 with dozens of races still uncalled. President Trump told the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal that he accepted the result and would work with Democrats.

Markets moved in two stages. As results came in during Asian hours, AI-related shares fell on expectations of tighter regulation of data centres, Treasury yields dipped and stock futures dropped. Once Trump's acceptance landed, equities rebounded hard: the S&P 500 rose 1.58 percent to 7,688.62, its best day in three months, and the Dow added more than 1,500 points.

The currency did not rebound with them. The dollar index touched 101.21, its lowest since 30 September, before closing around 101.48, down 0.38 percent on the day. The 10-year Treasury yield fell about six basis points to roughly 4.89 percent, having traded near 4.96 percent earlier in the session. Crude slipped as traders waited to see what the result meant for Washington's conduct of the war with Iran.

The logic is straightforward. A divided government means less fiscal expansion, less issuance and less inflationary pressure than a unified one, so the market priced fewer dollars needed and fewer reasons to hold them. Note what that makes this move: a repricing of American politics, not of the American economy.

What a US reader should take from this. Gridlock is usually read as market-friendly and the first two days supported that. The thing to watch is not the index level but the AI and data-centre exposure, which was the one sector that fell on the result rather than on the macro. If your work, your employer or your portfolio sits near that build-out, the regulatory environment just changed more than the rate environment did.

London used the opening to raise rates anyway

The Bank of England lifted Bank Rate to 4.00 percent, a quarter-point increase on an 8-1 vote at a meeting that ended on 4 November and was announced on Thursday. Catherine Mann dissented in favour of a half-point move to 4.25 percent. It is the first increase since August 2023 and the first change of any kind in 2026 after six consecutive holds.

The composition matters as much as the number. Swati Dhingra and Alan Taylor, both of whom had previously argued for holding, voted to raise. The doves moved.

The accompanying Monetary Policy Report was harder than the decision. Inflation is running at 3.5 percent against a 2 percent target, and Bank staff now expect it to reach about 5 percent by the middle of 2027. Governor Bailey tied that directly to the Middle East conflict and the energy prices coming out of it, and said he could see the argument for a further increase before taking a more conditional approach. Traders immediately raised bets on another move at the 17 December meeting.

Sterling had already been climbing. The Bank's trade-weighted index reached its highest reading since the day of the 2016 EU referendum, helped by the weaker dollar and by the expectation of exactly this decision.

What a UK reader should take from this. If you are on a tracker, your payment goes up this month. If you have a fixed rate ending in the next year, most lenders had already priced this hike in over the preceding week, so the quote you were given recently is probably still the one to take. The more important sentence is Bailey's. A central bank that tells you inflation is heading to 5 percent is not finished, and the December meeting is live.

Nairobi finally sold its thirty-year paper

Two weeks ago Kenyan Treasury bill demand collapsed to barely covering the offer, and the government went to market anyway asking for fifteen and thirty-year money. That auction closed on Wednesday.

It worked. Bids came to KSh99.47 billion against KSh50 billion offered, a performance rate of 198.9 percent, and the CBK accepted KSh70.86 billion. The thirty-year was the more popular of the two, drawing KSh62.40 billion in bids for a subscription of 124.8 percent, with KSh40.95 billion accepted. The fifteen-year drew KSh37.1 billion and took KSh29.90 billion.

The price tells the real story, and it is a story about duration rather than about Kenya. The fifteen-year cleared at a weighted average accepted yield of 12.7404 percent. The thirty-year cleared at 14.1266 percent. That is 139 basis points for twenty-two more years of the same borrower, in the same auction, on the same morning.

Against its 12.50 percent coupon, the thirty-year priced at KSh89.39 per KSh100, a deep discount to par and roughly 163 basis points above the coupon. That is expensive. It is also better than July, when the same bond cleared at 14.6166 percent and KSh88.57, though still above its April debut at 13.7554 percent.

The following day's bill auction drew KSh67.7 billion against KSh28 billion offered, a performance of 241.8 percent, and the pattern held: the 91-day took almost every shilling bid, while only about 34 percent of 182-day bids were accepted. The shilling sat at KSh129.96 to the dollar, unchanged from a week earlier, and banks' excess reserves averaged KSh31.9 billion above the cash reserve requirement.

What a Kenyan reader should take from this. The government can fund itself long, but it is paying 139 basis points to extend from fifteen years to thirty, and lenders are still crowding into three-month paper. For context on what that means for your own savings, the average published yield across 27 shilling money market funds sat at 9.14 percent. The government is paying fourteen for thirty-year money while you are offered nine for overnight money, and that gap is the price of duration in Kenya right now.

Lagos had the most to gain and lost ground

Nigeria is the market a weaker dollar should help most. It did not.

The naira closed at ₦1,346.01 on Monday and ₦1,349 on Friday, a depreciation of 0.22 percent across the week, with turnover across the four published sessions totalling about $1.45 billion. The CBN had not published Friday's volume at the time of writing.

The reason is domestic, not American. Banking system liquidity passed ₦7.7 trillion on 3 November after ₦2.016 trillion of OMO maturities settled, and the CBN spent the week pulling it back in. Across two auctions it offered ₦2.30 trillion, received ₦5.57 trillion of bids and allotted ₦3.46 trillion.

Read the two auctions against each other and the week explains itself. The CBN allotted roughly ₦2.95 trillion against a ₦1.50 trillion offer, taking nearly double what it asked for, at stop rates of 17.67 to 17.77 percent. The DMO, offering ₦800 billion the next day, received ₦2.045 trillion of bids and allotted ₦509.41 billion, holding stop rates at 15.40 percent on the 91-day, 15.79 on the 182-day and 15.80 on the 364-day. Even on the 364-day, where ₦1.994 trillion of the appetite went, it took ₦461.84 billion against a ₦600 billion offer.

So the central bank paid about 190 basis points more than the treasury for money in the same week, and roughly ₦1.54 trillion of Treasury bill bids went home unfilled. The government is not short of lenders. It is declining to pay what they are asking, and it can decline because the central bank is absorbing the liquidity at the higher rate instead. Average secondary-market bill yields still rose about 42 basis points to 17.70 percent from 17.28 percent a week earlier, which is where the market thinks the price actually is.

And the pressure is not over. The FMDA projects roughly ₦13.79 trillion of liquidity inflows during November, led by ₦8.927 trillion of maturing OMO bills, though it warns that fresh issuance will absorb a substantial part of that before it reaches anyone.

What a Nigerian reader should take from this. The naira is currently being set by how much money is sloshing around the banking system, not by what the dollar is doing in Washington. With ₦13.79 trillion due to land this month, expect the CBN to keep mopping and expect that to keep rates where they are rather than letting them fall. If you are lending to the government, the OMO window is paying almost two points more than the Treasury bill window for the privilege of lending to the same sovereign's central bank. The October inflation release lands on 16 November and the MPC meets on 23 and 24 November.

What actually happened this week

A shared tailwind arrived and produced four different outcomes.

Britain had the strongest hand and played it hardest: a cheaper dollar gave sterling room, and the Bank used that room to tighten into an energy shock rather than to relax. Kenya used the window to clear a long bond it had struggled to sell, and paid a visible premium for the privilege. Nigeria, the most dollar-sensitive of the four, got nothing from it, because its exchange rate is currently a function of domestic liquidity rather than external conditions. And the United States itself got the weaker currency as a by-product of a political result rather than an economic one.

The lesson is not that a weaker dollar does not matter. It is that it only reaches you if the channel between the dollar and your currency is open. In Britain that channel is wide. In Nigeria, ₦7.7 trillion of domestic liquidity is sitting in the way.

What would prove this wrong

If the naira strengthens through ₦1,330 in the next fortnight while system liquidity stays above ₦7 trillion, the domestic liquidity explanation is weaker than it looks and external conditions are doing more work than this reading allows. If the Bank of England holds on 17 December and the market unwinds its December pricing, Thursday's hawkishness was positioning rather than intent. If the dollar index recovers above 102.5 within two weeks, the post-election move was a positioning unwind rather than a repricing of US fiscal expectations. If Kenya's next long bond clears below 14 percent, the thirty-year has genuinely repriced tighter rather than simply recovering part of July's deterioration. And on the Nigerian reading specifically, if the DMO's next auction clears above 16 percent on the 364-day, the debt office was not declining to pay up so much as briefly able to avoid it.

Next move

  • CareerThe clearest signal this week was sectoral, not monetary. The one group of shares that fell on the US election result was AI and data centres, on the expectation that a Democratic Congress regulates the build-out more tightly. If you work in or around that build-out anywhere, including the engineering, construction, power and cooling roles underneath it, the political risk to those projects just rose, and it is worth knowing which of your employer's projects are already permitted. In Britain, a rate rise with another signalled means borrowing-sensitive sectors, housing, retail and construction, get harder from here, while energy and defence are where money is being committed. And the general rule holds: when central banks are raising rather than cutting, employers slow hiring decisions rather than cancel them, so expect processes to run long and keep more than one alive.
  • Business owners and operatorsIn Nigeria, do not plan around a stronger naira. It weakened in a week when the dollar fell globally, which tells you the currency is being driven by domestic liquidity, and ₦13.79 trillion more is coming this month. Price your imports and foreign payables at today's rate or worse, not at a hoped-for recovery. Borrowing costs are not falling either: stop rates were held and secondary yields rose about 42 basis points. In the UK, your cost of credit went up on Thursday and the Bank has signalled another move, so if you have a facility renewal or an invoice-finance line, deal with it before December. In Kenya, the government is paying over 14 percent for long money, which sets the floor your business competes against for bank lending; if you need credit, go now while banks are liquid.
  • InvestorsIn Nigeria the two windows are mispriced against each other: OMO cleared at 17.67 to 17.77 percent while Treasury bills cleared at 15.40 to 15.80, for the same sovereign in the same week, and ₦1.54 trillion of bill bids went unfilled. If you can reach the OMO window, that is where the yield is. In Kenya, the arithmetic is unusually stark: the government paid 14.13 percent for thirty-year money this week while the average shilling money market fund yields 9.14 percent. If you have been parking cash in a money market fund by default, that gap is worth understanding before you renew. In sterling, the hike is done and a December move is partly priced, so the easy part of the rate trade has passed. And in dollars, be careful reading one week's currency move as a trend: it came from an election result, and election-driven currency moves have a short memory.
