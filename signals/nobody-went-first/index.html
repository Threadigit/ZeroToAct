Nobody Went First

Last week lenders started pricing governments individually. This week the governments, the central banks and one very large company answered, and almost all of them answered the same way: not yet.

By Tolu Adetuyi

Last week lenders started pricing governments individually. This week the governments, the central banks and one very large company answered, and almost all of them answered the same way: not yet.

Frankfurt held and lost its president. Washington held and then cut the number of future chances it will have to move. London deferred the hard part of its Budget. In Abuja the debt office was offered ₦1.91 trillion and took ₦71 billion. And in New York, a Nigerian fintech held its listing ceremony, priced its shares and never traded them.

Frankfurt held, and its president resigned

The European Central Bank kept its deposit rate at 2.50 percent on Thursday. The Governing Council agreed unanimously, after discussing a further increase, with eurozone inflation running at 3.8 percent. Lagarde justified the hold partly by pointing at tighter financing conditions already being imposed by rising bond yields, which is a central bank saying the market has done the tightening for it.

Note what the hold is not. Markets came out of the meeting pricing a December increase at somewhere between two thirds and four fifths, depending on whose numbers you take. The next decision is 17 December. This was not a council that thinks the job is done. It is one that would rather move next time.

Hours before the decision, Lagarde announced she will step down on 7 February 2027, nine months ahead of a term due to run to October 2027. She told the Governing Council and the European Council president the same day, and said she wanted to be able to speak more freely on matters outside monetary policy. That phrasing has been read, in France in particular, as leaving a door open. We take no position on that.

The backdrop had improved slightly. Moody's left France at Aa3 on the previous Friday evening, concluding a periodic review without acting, against a widely expected downgrade to A+ that would have matched Fitch and S&P. It kept the negative outlook and conditioned the rating on a deficit-reducing 2027 budget. French bonds steadied.

None of that is resolution. France intends to issue around €340 billion next year. Asked whether the ECB would step in, Lagarde said it has tools if needed, which is the most a central bank can say without committing to anything.

Washington held, then reduced its own chances to act

The Fed left the federal funds target range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent on Wednesday. The vote was 10-2. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both dissented in favour of a quarter-point increase. The statement upgraded its description of economic activity from solid to strong and repeated that inflation remains elevated.

The hold was expected. Two things were not.

The first was who dissented. Cook had never dissented on a monetary policy decision before. Two voters wanting to tighten, in a committee that cut through 2025, is a tightening bias on the record, and the market read it that way: odds of a December increase moved to around 80 percent.

The second was procedural, and it matters more than it sounds. Warsh disclosed that from next autumn the FOMC will meet less often to set rates, with some meetings given over to broad strategy and carrying no rate decision or statement. A central bank with inflation at 3.7 percent and two members voting to hike has just reduced the number of scheduled opportunities it will have to respond. Each remaining meeting now carries more weight and the gaps between them are longer.

Asked whether the midterms, six days away, had influenced the decision, Warsh said politics played no role.

London deferred the bill

John Healey delivered his first Budget as Chancellor on Wednesday and the market's verdict was relief rather than enthusiasm. Gilts edged higher and sterling barely moved.

He kept the previous fiscal rules and around £15 billion of headroom. The OBR put debt at 83.8 percent of GDP next year, falling to 81.7 by 2032, raised growth forecasts to 1.2 percent this year and 1.0 next, and added £14 billion to this year's debt interest bill. Revenue came from banks rather than wages, with the surcharge going from 3 to 6 percent for fifteen months from January. Energy bills were cut, with £100 to households in council tax bands A to D and gas standing charges scrapped, and defence spending was fully funded.

The real trade is in the issuance remit rather than the speech. The Debt Management Office cut long-dated gilt sales by £6.9 billion to £15.5 billion, roughly a third, while lifting total 2026/27 issuance by £4.4 billion to £250.6 billion. That leaves the long end at about 6 percent of the programme, the smallest share since 1990. Borrow the same money, borrow less of it where it costs most, and leave the structural questions for later. Analysts put what was deferred at up to £40 billion next year.

Markets had priced a Bank of England increase on 5 November at roughly 99 percent before the Budget, and the Budget did not change that.

A company rang the bell and never traded

On Friday morning OPay held its listing ceremony at the New York Stock Exchange. Its co-chief executive described the moment as a milestone for the company and for Nigeria.

The shares never traded.

The sequence is worth setting out precisely, because it is unusually compressed. OPay filed its F-1 with the SEC on 9 October. The NYSE approved the listing on 27 October under the ticker OPAY. The underwriting agreement was dated 29 October. The company priced 16 million American depositary shares at $19 each, the bottom of a $19 to $21 range, raising $304 million and implying a valuation near $4 billion, with the shares due to open on 30 October. Citigroup and Deutsche Bank led the offering; reports differ on the third bank. Standard Bank's Stanbic unit had agreed a private placement of up to $200 million tied to the IPO closing.

On the day, the exchange halted the stock before it opened, with news pending. Hours later the company and its underwriters mutually agreed to terminate the underwriting agreement, confirmed on 31 October.

OPay cited a regulatory inquiry in a foreign jurisdiction it did not name, describing that jurisdiction as immaterial to its core operations and saying it is confident the inquiry will be concluded satisfactorily in the near future. It said not proceeding at this time was the most prudent course of action, that the inquiry should not be read as a reflection on its business performance, financial condition, operational integrity or regulatory standing, and that services in Nigeria and its other markets continue normally. A secondary listing on the Nigerian Exchange had been planned to follow. No new date has been given for either.

We are reporting the public record and nothing beyond it. The jurisdiction has not been named, the nature of the inquiry has not been disclosed, and we are not going to speculate about either. OPay is a client of Prembly, where the author is a co-founder.

What can be said is structural. A company can satisfy the SEC, satisfy the NYSE, assemble bulge-bracket underwriters, price a book and hold the ceremony, and still not open, because of an open question in a market it considers small. For any business operating across several countries, the smallest jurisdiction on the licence list is now capable of gating the largest transaction on the calendar.

Three weeks ago Airtel Money listed in London and opened below its offer price. This week OPay priced in New York and did not open at all. Those are two different problems, and together they describe a window for African listings that is open but narrow.

Abuja was offered ₦1.91 trillion and took ₦71 billion

Nigeria produced the week's most striking number and almost nobody outside Lagos noticed.

The Debt Management Office offered ₦800 billion of Treasury bills on Wednesday. Subscriptions came to ₦1.91 trillion, a bid-to-cover of 2.38 times. The DMO allotted ₦71.16 billion, roughly nine percent of what it had advertised, rejecting about 96 percent of the bids placed with it. The one-year stop rate fell to 15.80 percent, with the 91-day at 15.40 and the 182-day at 15.79.

Read that again. An issuer with a fourth-quarter funding programme running into trillions turned down more than ₦1.8 trillion of offered money because it did not like the price, and in doing so pushed its own borrowing rate down. The demand was concentrated where the rejection was hardest: the 364-day alone drew ₦1.84 trillion against a ₦600 billion offer.

It could do that because the system is awash. Banks held ₦6.98 trillion at the standing deposit facility on Tuesday. Average secondary-market Treasury bill yields had already fallen 75 basis points to 17.47 percent by Monday. Reserves reached $55.26 billion, an eighteen-year high and 28.3 percent above a year earlier, though the governor puts net reserves nearer $46 billion. The naira, which had slipped to 1,367.61 on Monday, recovered to 1,346.91 by Thursday after central bank intervention, while the parallel market stayed near 1,385.

Two weeks ago Nigerian auctions were clearing at rising rates. This week the issuer refused the market's price and got its own. That is the opposite of what happened in Paris, and it is the same question.

Nairobi refused to lend long

Kenyan Treasury bills drew KSh51.9 billion against KSh28 billion offered on Thursday, a performance rate of 185.2 percent, recovering from barely covered the week before.

But the recovery was not evenly spread. The 91-day attracted the bulk of it. Both the 182-day and the 364-day came in below the amounts offered. Commercial banks' excess reserves averaged KSh32.6 billion above the 3.25 percent cash reserve requirement during the week, and the interbank rate sat unchanged at 8.75 percent.

So the money came back and it went to the shortest instrument on the curve, again. Kenyan investors are willing to lend to their government for three months and reluctant to do it for six or twelve.

What actually happened this week

Last week's Signal argued that lenders had begun pricing governments individually rather than as a class. This week was the reply, and the reply was a refusal to settle.

The Fed held and then gave itself fewer chances to act. The ECB held and lost the person who would have had to act. The Chancellor met the market's expectations precisely by not resolving anything, and the DMO quietly shifted issuance away from the expensive end of the curve. Nigeria's debt office declined the price on offer and walked away from ₦1.8 trillion. Kenya's investors declined to go past three months. And OPay, having done everything required of it, stopped at the last step.

Four of those were choices. OPay's was not, which is the point worth holding onto: deferral is comfortable until it is imposed on you.

Nobody wanted to be the one who moved first, and in most of these cases that is a defensible choice. But the gap between what lenders now want and what borrowers will pay did not close this week. It was carried forward, and the calendar is short: the Bank of England on 5 November, the Fed in December, the ECB on 17 December, and Nigeria and Kenya at every auction in between. Both the Fed and the ECB left the meeting with a December move priced at better than two thirds. Not yet is not the same as no.

Deferral is cheap while liquidity is abundant. Nigeria could reject 96 percent of bids because its banks are sitting on ₦6.98 trillion. Kenya's auction recovered because its banks hold excess reserves. When that liquidity thins, the same refusals cost real money.

Method

Written on 1 November 2026, covering 25 to 31 October. Every anchor falls inside that window. ECB figures are from the 29 October decision and press conference; the Lagarde announcement was made the same day. The December pricing for both the ECB and the Fed is market-implied and the sources differ, so it is given as a range rather than a point. Moody's decision on the French rating was taken on the evening of 23 October, outside this window, and is included only as context for the market's behaviour on 26 October, which is inside it. The French spread levels in the chart are benchmark 10-year yields; the 22 October figure is the widest reported close of that week, as given in the previous edition, and intraday prints that week ranged lower. Fed figures are from the FOMC statement and the Warsh press conference of 28 October. UK figures are from the Budget of 28 October, OBR forecasts published the same day, and the DMO's revised financing remit of the same date; the long-end share and its comparison to 1990 are from Reuters reporting of that remit. OPay details are from the company's own statements of 30 and 31 October as carried by Reuters and the Nigerian press, together with its SEC filing history and the NYSE listing approval; reports disagree on which bank joined Citigroup and Deutsche Bank on the offering, so only those two are named here. The jurisdiction conducting the inquiry has not been named publicly and the nature of the inquiry has not been disclosed; this edition reports only what is on the public record and draws no inference about either. OPay is a client of Prembly, where the author is a co-founder. Nigerian figures are from the DMO Treasury bill auction of 28 October, with liquidity and reserve data for 26 to 29 October. Kenyan figures are from the CBK weekly bulletin dated 30 October covering the 29 October auction; the bulletin gives the headline performance rate and says the two longer tenors came in under offer, without publishing tenor-level amounts. The Bank of England decision on 5 November, the US midterms on 3 November, the Kenyan bond close on 4 November and the ECB decision on 17 December are scheduled future events. Commentary on whether Lagarde's stated wish to speak more freely implies a political move is attributed speculation, not fact, and we take no position on it.

What would prove this wrong

The easy read is that a week of holds means a cycle has stalled. It may mean the opposite, since both the Fed and the ECB left December priced at better than two thirds. Five things would weaken this edition. If the Bank of England hikes on 5 November and the gilt curve steepens rather than rallies, the market reads the Budget as under-delivering and this week's calm was temporary. If Nigeria's next auction forces the DMO to take materially more than it wants at rates back above 16 percent, the rejection was a one-off supported by unusual liquidity rather than a durable pricing position. If the French spread over Germany widens back through 175 basis points before the ECB's December meeting, Moody's reprieve bought nothing and the deferral was the expensive kind. If Kenya's 182-day and 364-day come in oversubscribed at the next two auctions, the short-end crowding was a timing effect rather than a view on duration. And on OPay the test is time and price: if the offering is revived within a quarter at or near $19, the inquiry was procedural and the company's characterisation holds; if it is still withdrawn past the first quarter of 2027, or returns at a materially lower price, the market will have concluded otherwise regardless of what anyone says now.

Next move

  • CareerYou do not need to work in finance for this week to reach you, because institutional deferral becomes employer deferral. When central banks and treasuries decide to wait, the companies that borrow from them wait too, and that shows up as hiring decisions that slip a quarter. If you are in a process right now, expect it to take longer than you were told, keep other conversations alive, and do not read a slow reply as a no. Where money actually moved is more useful than where it did not. Britain's Budget funded defence and cut energy bills while putting the bill on banks, doubling their surcharge to 6 percent for fifteen months, which is a straightforward statement about which British sectors will be adding roles next year and which will be managing costs. In Nigeria, reserves at an eighteen-year high, a naira that recovered through the week and government borrowing costs falling is the first stretch in a while where importers, manufacturers and anyone with a foreign supplier can plan a budget that survives the quarter; those are the businesses that resume hiring first after a currency scare, and they tend to do it quietly before they advertise. One more, for anyone at a company that talks about going public: OPay reached its listing ceremony and its shares still did not trade, so if part of why you took a job is an expected listing and what it would do for your shares, value the job on what it pays you now. And the universal one. If your personal plan was waiting for borrowing to get cheaper, whether that is a house, a car, a course or going out on your own, every institution that could have brought that date closer this week chose to wait instead, and two of them have a rise priced for December. Plan at today's cost of money rather than next year's, and if the plan only works at a lower rate, it is not a plan yet.
  • Business owners and operatorsNigerian borrowing costs moved in your favour this week. The one-year stop rate fell to 15.80 percent and secondary yields dropped 75 basis points, which means commercial paper and bank facilities reprice down with a lag. If you were quoted in early October, go back and ask again now. In the UK, the Budget raised the bank surcharge to 6 percent for fifteen months, and banks do not absorb that quietly; expect it in pricing and covenants on new facilities, and if you were close to signing, sign. In the US, the Fed has given itself fewer scheduled chances to cut, so plan on current rates persisting rather than easing. And across all four markets, nothing this week reduced energy costs or the cost of long-term money, so any plan that depends on either falling next year is still exposed. The OPay withdrawal carries a separate lesson for anyone operating in more than one country. A single unresolved regulatory question, in a market the company itself calls immaterial, stopped a $304 million transaction after it had been priced. Your licence position in your smallest market is now a gating item for your largest deal, your next raise and your eventual exit. If you have dormant entities, lapsed filings or a jurisdiction nobody has looked at in two years, that is this quarter's work, not next year's.
  • InvestorsThe Nigerian rejection is the most interesting event of the week for a domestic investor, and it cuts against you. The issuer has shown it will refuse your price when it does not need your money, so the window to lock naira yields above 16 percent is closing while liquidity stays this heavy. Act on that or accept lower. In Kenya, the market keeps telling you what it thinks of duration by refusing the 182-day and 364-day, and the 15-year and 30-year reopening closing 4 November is where that gets tested properly. In sterling, the Budget removed a tail risk rather than creating an opportunity, and a 99 percent priced hike on 5 November is already in the gilt price; the more interesting detail is that the long end is now the smallest share of the programme since 1990, which is a statement about who the government thinks will lend to it and for how long. In dollars, note the structural change: fewer scheduled Fed meetings means fewer scheduled chances to be rescued, and that should widen the range of outcomes you plan for rather than narrow it. For anyone who was waiting on OPay's planned secondary listing in Lagos, that is now on hold with no date. Two of the largest African listings attempted this year have gone the same way, one opening below its offer in London and one not opening at all in New York. Execution risk, rather than appetite, is the binding constraint, and that is a reason to be sceptical of any valuation that assumes a public exit on a particular date.
